This book, in great detail, convincingly shows that markets are the most effective way to "allocate resources to people with stronger needs and interests", and explains the core problem of our capitalistic, market-based society: markets are currently not fair/open (e.g., wealthy hoarding), or lacking entirely where they should exist (e.g., some government services).

It then proposed five radical solutions to the symptoms of the problems, opening each section with an example of their solution in action (which I won't detail):

1. For property (and assets): a self-imposed valuation that's taxable, paired with the requirement to sell if someone offers to buy at the set price.
  1. It ensures that property (and assets) are valued and taxed appropriately, and the market is the best mechanism to set that price.
2. For asymmetrical political representation: Quadratic voting
  1. Elected politicians, and the general public, can express *how much* they care about an issue, not just yes/no.
3. A migration system that is overly restrictive: auction + sponsorship
  1. A citizen can pay to sponsor someone who wants to work for them.
4. Institutional investors that own a large portion of the market: regulation
  1. Some firms like BlackRock and Vanguard own a significant share of *most* companies in a particular sector, which can lead to non-competitive incentives.
5. Data providers (e.g., social media users): get paid for contributions, increasingly important in the AI age. 

They concluded the book by hypothesizing how all five solutions could be paired together for even greater positive effect, concluding that radical markets would improve wellbeing for all, reduce inequality and discrimination, and build trust within and across borders.

I was quite convinced of their problem statement and definition about markets being the most efficient way to elicit information from a group of people. I was also very convinced by parts of the first and all of the second proposed solution. 

Treating property as a speculative investment has blocked most of my generation from access to affordable housing in much of the world, and unfairly distorts wealth and politics simply because someone was old, lucky, or wealthy enough to buy in at a lower price point. The system they proposed would lower property values to their true value, making housing more affordable and redistributing the wealth more effectively in the process. With so much of a nation's wealth tied up in its property values, this was a strong argument that lowering the total value would actually be a good thing for *everyone* except the very top 0.0X%.

> Capitalism liberated many from feudalism, radical markets would further liberate from wealth privilege.
